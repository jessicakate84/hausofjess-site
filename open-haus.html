To VIR or not to VIR

We spend months getting pricing right for the market. Then the asks start: vendor incentive rebates (VIRs), growth rebates, marketing agreements, co-op funds, and programs passed down the channel to the end customer. Each one sounds reasonable on its own. Stack them up, and the margin you fought for is quietly gone.

I'm not against incentives. Used well, they're one of the strongest levers in the channel. The problem is handing them out as a cost of doing business, instead of trading them for something.

Here's the trade I want. If we're investing in your growth, we want to be the anchor tenant in your line card for our category: the vendor you lead with, train your team on and build your program around. Committed volume, clear targets, a shared plan. In return, the incentives scale as you grow, and we grow together.

That's what a real partnership looks like. Both sides have skin in the game. If a partner won't commit, the incentive isn't buying loyalty. It's just a discount with paperwork.

Business is still personal

We have more ways than ever to reach a customer: AI-written emails, automated sequences, CRMs that log every click, and marketing so polished it could be about anyone. That's the problem. Too often, it is about anyone.

The old-school tactics still work, maybe better than ever, because so few people bother. A dinner where nobody opens a laptop. A handwritten thank-you note. A call on the anniversary of a project, just to ask how it's going. Remembering their kid's name, their dog's name, their favorite steakhouse.

I'm not against the tools. Let them handle the busywork, so your people have more time for the human part, not less. The CRM should remind you to send the note. It shouldn't write it.

Loyalty and trust are built between people, not platforms. It's what the whole flywheel comes down to: how does it feel to do business with you?

Progress beats perfect

Good ideas rarely die from bad execution. They die in boardrooms, picked apart before they ever ship. FFS, communicate clearly. Leadership means speaking plainly, setting expectations and direction, and moving quickly. Take the feedback, adapt, and keep the wheel turning.

Your first version shouldn't feel finished, and that's fine if you're honest about it. Tell your first clients exactly where things stand: we're building this now, we see you as an early adopter, and your feedback will shape it around your business. People don't mind being early. They mind being misled.

Then keep changing. A program that stops evolving goes stale against the market, and your competitors will notice before you do. Protect the core that isn't up for debate, and refine everything around it.

Here's what most leaders miss: clients don't expect perfect on day one. What earns their trust is watching you tailor the program to them, week by week.

The Ritz-Carlton standard

I'll admit it: I'm a Ritz-Carlton loyalist. I got into a friendly debate about hotel preferences the other day, and I always end up in the same place. When I walk into a Ritz, I know things will be taken care of. And if something does go wrong, they'll make it right, without me having to chase it.

A couple of years ago I sailed with the Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection. There was close to one crew member for every guest, and everyone knew my name. It was one of the most profound service experiences of my life, and it wasn't the yacht that made it. It was how personal it felt.

That feeling isn't luck. It's engineered. Ritz-Carlton trains every employee to hunt five kinds of defect, and gives them a name that's easy to remember. It's part of the quality system behind the company's two Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Awards.

MR. BIV
  1. M
    Mistakes

    Errors in the work itself. The wrong part ships, the quote carries the wrong price, a detail gets missed in the spec.

  2. R
    Rework

    Doing it twice because it wasn't right the first time. Re-quoting, re-shipping, sending a technician back out. Every bit of rework is margin you already earned, spent again.

  3. B
    Breakdowns

    A system, a piece of equipment or a handoff fails. The install team never got the drawings. The follow-up never fired.

  4. I
    Inefficiencies

    Wasted time, steps or effort. Five approvals for a standard quote. Three people chasing the same answer.

  5. V
    Variation

    The same service, delivered differently depending on who does it or when. This is the dangerous one. One inconsistent experience undoes a dozen flawless ones, because customers remember the exception, not the average.

In technology sales, most of MR. BIV hides in the handoffs: sales to install, install to support, one rep's promise to another rep's delivery. Nobody owns the gap, so nobody fixes it.

What makes the system work is that spotting defects is everyone's job, and the blame goes to the process, not the person. That's how a premium brand earns its premium.

Try it this week. Pick one handoff in your business and walk it with a pen. Write down every M, R, B, I and V you find. You'll have your list of fixes by Friday.

Silence is data

Nobody wants another survey, and nobody wants to log into your portal. So stop asking customers how you're doing, and start reading what they do. How fast do they reply? Is the tone warming or cooling? Are senior people still showing up, or have they quietly handed you down the org chart? Silence is data.

Flip the direction too. Push updates, health reports and results to the customer before they ask. A quarterly check-in from someone senior, with no agenda beyond “how are we doing for you?”, tells you more than any form. Log every interaction green, amber or red, and the picture builds itself.

First principles beat best practice

Most pricing, channel and product decisions are made by analogy: this is the market price, this is how competitors sell, this is how it's always been done. First-principles thinking strips a problem back to what's actually true, then rebuilds from there.

Take price. Instead of “this is what the category costs,” list the real inputs: components, labor, freight, channel margin. Which are fixed by physics, and which are only fixed by habit? The gap between the two is where new business models live.

Channel works the same way. Instead of copying the incumbent's distribution model, ask what each layer actually adds. It's slow work, so save it for the stuck, high-stakes problems.

Distribution's value is moving

Distribution used to win on logistics, credit and the breadth of its line card. Those advantages are eroding. Marketplaces handle logistics, manufacturers extend credit directly, and online configurators replace a lot of pre-sales engineering.

What's left to own is the capability layer: enablement, services, financing structures, recurring revenue infrastructure and vertical solution design. The distributors that win the next decade will wholesale capabilities to their partners, not just boxes.

And the real competition isn't the distributor across town. It's IT distribution moving into AV, manufacturer-direct programs, and software platforms that treat hardware as an afterthought.

Twenty committed partners beat two hundred casual ones

Most partner programs measure success by headcount: how many dealers signed up. But a partner who signed a form and never sold you is a cost, not an asset.

One idea I've been testing is making certification itself a product. Partners pay an annual fee for real value: platform access, co-branded marketing, shared leads in their territory and priority support for their clients. Two things happen. You earn revenue before they close their first deal, and you filter for partners serious enough to invest.

Own a vertical

The most defensible position in any market is being the undisputed expert in a specific vertical. Horizontal offers are easy to compare and easy to undercut. Deep vertical expertise isn't.

Pick two or three verticals where customers have multiple locations, recurring budgets and a need for brand consistency. In display technology, think fitness studios, quick-service restaurants and houses of worship. Then go so deep, with content, references, pricing and language built for that vertical, that no generalist can touch you.

The financing spread nobody talks about

When a customer finances a project, the finance company keeps the spread between the contract's face value and what it pays you. On larger deals, that's real money leaving the building.

As volume grows, you gain leverage: better advance rates first, and eventually perhaps a captive finance program for smaller deals. Copier dealers and equipment rental companies figured this out years ago. It takes capital and regulatory care, but it's how a product business starts to look like a financial services business.
