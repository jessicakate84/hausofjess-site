Upstairs at the Haus

A look at life outside the boardroom: travel, music, art, and now and then the quiet part said out loud.

Downstairs is business. Up here is everything else: the places I love, the things that keep me steady, and what it's actually like to walk into the room as me.

Home base

Deep Ellum

Home is Deep Ellum, Dallas’s original music district, and it suits me. Strong coffee by morning, crates of vinyl by afternoon, live music loud enough to rattle your ribs by night. It’s loud, it’s creative, it’s unapologetically itself, and its history runs deeper than most cities’. Murals on every wall, a band on every corner, and not once has it asked permission to be fabulous. Honestly? Same.

Sound

Every genre, on repeat

Gospel raised my standards: Fred Hammond, Kirk Franklin, and Hezekiah Walker and the Love Fellowship Crusade Choir, still undefeated. Since then it’s been country, my friend Jenni’s punk band Tomgirls, New York house, Taylor, and Olivia Dean on a loop I refuse to apologize for. At fourteen I agonized over East Coast or West Coast. A good bass line still gets me dancing.

Travel

Comfort, not status

Main cabin or up front, I’m flexible. But I’m tall, darling, so legroom isn’t a luxury. It’s survival. Perfect travel: laptop closed, earbuds in, great music on. Overnight? Face mask, under-eye patches and a cheeky glass of wine. Arrive rested, and look like it. And when I get to choose where I sleep, it’s a Ritz-Carlton. Every time.

Art

Art & adventures

Wherever I land, I go looking for art, the kind that stops you mid-step. In London a few weeks ago, Paul Delaroche’s The Execution of Lady Jane Grey did exactly that at the National Gallery. It spoke to me in a way I’m still unpacking, and every trip is better with a gallery stop. There’s a whole piece coming on why that painting got me.

Read my full take

Why do we like what we like? Pierre Bourdieu’s answer, built from large surveys of French life in the 1960s, is uncomfortable: taste is neither as personal nor as innocent as it feels.

At the center of the book is the habitus: a set of deep, mostly unconscious dispositions we absorb from the conditions we grow up in, through family, schooling and class. It shapes what feels natural to us: how we dress, eat, speak, decorate and carry our bodies, even what we find beautiful. Because it’s learned so early, it feels like instinct. That’s the trick. What looks like natural good taste is upbringing and education, worn so comfortably that it passes for personality.

Bourdieu maps society as a space of capital. Economic capital is money. Cultural capital is knowledge, education and cultivated taste. Social capital is who you know. Different groups hold different mixes, and their tastes line up with them. People far from economic necessity develop a taste of freedom: form over function, the ability to admire something for its own sake. People closer to necessity favor the practical, the substantial and the generous. In between sits a middle class working hard to learn the rules.

His most famous line: Taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier. Every choice of style places you in the social order, whether you mean it to or not.

That’s why fashion never stands still. Style is a system of differences. Once a look spreads widely, it loses its power to distinguish, and those richest in cultural capital move on to something rarer. Distinction is always relative.

Why I’m reading it: every brand is a habitus in miniature. It signals who it’s for and who it isn’t, mostly without saying a word. If you want to understand why customers pay for a name, or why a premium brand guards its rarity, start here.
